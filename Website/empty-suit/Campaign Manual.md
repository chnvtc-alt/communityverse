# The Empty Suit Campaign Manual

This is a living guide for players who want to understand how the primary works, how the map behaves, and how to make better strategic choices. It is written for the current build and should be updated as the game evolves.

## What Kind of Game This Is

The Empty Suit is a compressed presidential primary simulator. It is not just about picking the strongest candidate and clicking through days. The game is built around reading the map correctly, deciding where a lead is safe or soft, and balancing today’s needs against what is about to open next.

The campaign is not won by doing one thing well. It is won by combining message discipline, state targeting, money, timing, and narrative management.

## Choosing A Candidate

Your first strategic choice is the candidate. Each one comes into the race with a different political lane, different natural fits on the map, and different strengths in the hidden support model.

When choosing a candidate, think about three things:

1. How easy is this candidate to understand?
2. How broad is this candidate’s natural coalition?
3. How punishing is the map if you make mistakes with them?

### Joseph Pepper

Joseph Pepper is the coalition-keeper type. He tends to be steadier, more institutional, and better suited to voters who value electability, labor credibility, and a grounded tone. He is usually a good pick if you want a more stable campaign with fewer ideological edges.

Good for players who want:
- a safer opening lane
- less reliance on media theatrics
- a campaign that can survive a few imperfect rounds

### Wicked Jim DeVito

Wicked Jim is more machine-coded and combative. He can work in places that reward hardball politics and insider muscle, but he is not naturally loved by more idealistic or reform-heavy electorates.

Good for players who want:
- a rougher, more tactical run
- to win through pressure rather than broad affection
- a campaign where image management matters a lot

### Curtis Coolwater

Curtis Coolwater is a reform-minded, media-friendly candidate with stronger reach in diverse and fast-moving states. He is often a good fit if you want a flexible map candidate who can catch waves when momentum turns.

Good for players who want:
- stronger upside in fluid states
- a modern, message-driven campaign
- a candidate who can benefit from good timing and broad narrative shifts

### Camila Olivia Acosta

Camila Olivia Acosta is the most movement-progressive of the group. She is strongest where youth vote, progressive activism, reform appetite, and ideological energy all matter. She can be very dangerous if the map is giving her the right terrain, but she may require more deliberate handling in electability-heavy states.

Good for players who want:
- a sharper ideological lane
- strong upside in progressive terrain
- to build wins around energy and movement politics

### General Candidate-Pick Advice

- Pick Joseph Pepper if you want the most forgiving learning curve.
- Pick Curtis Coolwater if you want a candidate with broad upside and room to improvise.
- Pick Camila Olivia Acosta if you want a more distinctive ideological map and are comfortable with sharper tradeoffs.
- Pick Wicked Jim DeVito if you want a harder, more adversarial-feeling campaign.

## How The Primary Calendar Works

The primary is compressed into a limited number of windows. Each window may contain multiple playable days and multiple states. Some states are live now. Others are on deck and matter because the best campaigns are already planning for them before they fully open.

This means you should never look only at the state that is voting today. You should also ask:

- What opens next?
- Which current states are actually winnable?
- Which on-deck states can be shaped cheaply before everyone piles in?

## Live States Vs On-Deck States

Live states are the places where your campaign actions matter most immediately. On-deck states are tomorrow’s problem, but the smart player often starts working them before the rest of the field fully commits.

A good campaign usually mixes:
- immediate work in one or two real battlegrounds
- light shaping of on-deck states
- enough flexibility to pivot if the map changes

If you wait until a state is fully live before touching it, you may already be late.

## How To Read Polls

Polls are not promises. A lead can look solid and still be fake.

When reading a state, pay attention to:

- the raw margin
- the undecided vote
- the margin of error
- whether the state is being described as a soft lead, stable lead, toss-up, reachable, or behind

The important idea is this:

- A lead with a lot of undecided vote is fragile.
- A narrow lead inside the margin of error is not a lock.
- A state where you trail but a lot of voters are still movable may be more useful than a state where you lead narrowly but almost no one is left to persuade.

## Soft Leads Vs Safe Leads

A soft lead is one of the most dangerous positions in the game. It tempts you to leave too early.

A lead is softer when:
- the margin is small
- undecideds are still high
- the polling error leaves room for a reversal

A lead is safer when:
- the margin is real
- undecideds are low
- the state is closer to resolution

The campaign manager may warn you when a lead is not actually secure. Pay attention to that warning.

## Delegates

Delegates are awarded by party primary results, not by national poll position. You are trying to build an actual delegate path, not just look strong on the screen.

Important habits:
- value big delegate states, but do not ignore smaller states if they are efficient wins
- do not spend huge resources just to barely lose expensively
- understand that a narrow win can still be valuable if it banks delegates and momentum

## Message Mix

Your message mix is what the candidate talks about that day. It helps determine fit with different states and shapes how your campaign is being interpreted.

The right message mix is not always your favorite issue stack. It should reflect:
- what the live map wants
- what your candidate is naturally good at
- whether you are trying to persuade, consolidate, or sharpen contrast

If your lead issue is working, keep using it. But if every state starts reading your campaign as one-note, you may need a secondary issue that broadens the frame.

## Building A Brand Over Time

Your campaign is not judged only by what you talk about today. Over time, the game tracks the issues you keep returning to and builds a longer-term picture of what your candidate stands for.

Think of it this way:
- today’s message mix affects today’s rallies, campaigning, and ad choices
- repeated issue choices gradually build your campaign’s political brand
- that brand helps shape how naturally your candidate fits future states

This means repetition matters. If you spend several windows leaning heavily into Economy and Healthcare, your candidate starts to read more like an Economy-and-Healthcare candidate on the broader map. That does not mean every state suddenly becomes yours, but it does mean later state fit can tilt a little more in that direction.

This is usually a good thing when:
- you have found a lane that matches your candidate
- the map keeps rewarding the same kind of message
- you want voters to start seeing a clear identity instead of a vague, shapeless campaign

It becomes a risk when:
- you never widen the frame
- every day sounds identical
- the campaign starts feeling one-note while the map is asking for something else

Example:

If Joseph Pepper spends several rounds leading with Economy, backing it with Healthcare, and keeping Unity as a secondary lane, he gradually builds a kitchen-table, coalition-keeper brand. That can help him feel more natural in labor-heavy or cost-of-living states later on.

If, after all that, he suddenly spends one day going hard on Immigration, that can still help with that day’s tactics. But it will not instantly erase the broader brand he has already built. One off-message day can matter. Repetition matters more.

The practical rule is:
- do not thrash your message every turn
- repeat enough to build a recognizable lane
- keep at least one secondary issue available so the campaign does not narrow into a caricature

## Route Planning

The itinerary system is really about concentration versus spread.

Lock-in style routes are best when:
- one state is clearly winnable
- you need to close hard
- you cannot afford to dilute effort

Broader routes are best when:
- several states are close enough to move
- the window is wide
- you need to stay present across multiple places at once

Do not spread out just because the board is large. Spread out only when you have enough value targets to justify it.

## Rallies, General Campaigning, Endorsements, And Fundraising

These actions do different jobs.

### Rallies

Rallies are your high-visibility public stops. They can create sharper support movement and enthusiasm in specific states, but they also cost real effort and contribute to fatigue.

Use rallies when:
- a state is close
- your candidate fits the audience
- you need a visible closing push

### General Campaigning

General campaigning is your broad day-to-day field and media presence. It is less dramatic than a rally, but it is often the glue that makes the day work.

Use it when:
- you want steady lift
- your route includes several useful states
- you need a less theatrical but still meaningful push

### Endorsement Work

Endorsement work is a validator play. It is usually not explosive, but it can be efficient, especially in states where local credibility matters.

Use it when:
- you need trust more than spectacle
- your candidate benefits from validators
- a state is persuadable but not naturally yours

### Fundraising

Fundraising does two things:
- it gives you money
- it can also affect next-turn narrative and momentum

That means it is not just “skip the map and make cash.” A good fundraising day can help position the campaign. But if you overdo it, you may underwork the actual board and pay for that later.

## Advertising

Advertising is how you reach multiple places without consuming candidate time. It becomes more important as the map gets wider.

The main question is not whether to advertise. It is how concentrated to be.

Local buys are best when:
- a state is very close
- you have a clear target
- you want efficient pressure in one place

Window-wide buys are best when:
- the window is large
- you need broad presence
- the board is too wide to cover only through in-person activity

Ads should support a strategy, not replace one. Buying media everywhere is usually too expensive. Buying nowhere can leave you absent from the map.

## Momentum

Momentum is a narrative and campaign-performance measure. It helps represent whether your campaign is rising, stalling, or taking damage in the political conversation.

Momentum can be affected by:
- how well you perform relative to expectations
- how your fundraising reads
- how well your daily activity is moving states
- whether you close strong or disappoint

Momentum matters because it can shape how future rounds unfold. But it is not the same as delegates, and it is not the same as favorability.

## Favorability

Favorability is closer to the candidate’s public image and general political health. It is a broader “what do voters think of this person” measure than momentum.

You can think of it this way:
- momentum is “how is the campaign going right now?”
- favorability is “how is the candidate being received overall?”

A candidate can have decent momentum but weak favorability, or vice versa. Home-state losses, overperformance, disappointment, and negative campaigning can all matter here.

## Fatigue

Fatigue reflects how worn down a campaign schedule is making the candidate. A very aggressive schedule can still work, but over time fatigue makes execution weaker and increases the risk that a candidate stops getting full value from their day.

Do not ignore fatigue completely. But do not treat it as a reason to stop campaigning. The goal is not to avoid fatigue. The goal is to spend it intelligently.

## Enthusiasm

Enthusiasm is a local energy measure. It reflects whether a candidate’s supporters in a state are engaged and activated.

Higher enthusiasm can help a candidate hold territory more effectively and get more from campaign actions. It is especially useful in states where your campaign already fits naturally and you want that support to stay sticky.

## How To Approach The Early Game

In the early game, the most common mistake is trying to win everything.

A better approach is:
- identify your natural states
- identify one or two places where the field is still movable
- invest enough to establish a story without draining your entire treasury

The early game is about position, not total conquest.

## How To Approach The Midgame

By the middle of the primary, the board gets wider and the temptation to overreact gets stronger. This is where players often lose discipline.

Good midgame habits:
- keep separating live states from on-deck states
- do not chase every close poll
- use ads to widen reach when in-person coverage becomes too thin
- let your strongest issue carry the frame, but avoid becoming too predictable

## How To Approach Super Tuesday

Super Tuesday is where map management and money management become inseparable.

You usually cannot do everything. So decide which of these you are trying to do:
- protect a lead
- score selective wins
- stay alive everywhere enough to avoid collapse
- build a broad narrative wave

You do not need every state. You need the right mix of delegate value, winnability, and future positioning.

## Common Mistakes

- Leaving a soft lead too early
- Spending heavily in states with almost no path
- Ignoring on-deck states until they are expensive
- Overfundraising while the live board slips away
- Running a message mix that does not match the states that matter
- Mistaking a good-looking poll for a settled state
- Treating momentum as if it were delegates

## Final Advice

The game rewards players who can tell the difference between:
- being ahead and being safe
- being active and being efficient
- making noise and actually building a delegate path

The best campaigns are usually not the loudest ones. They are the ones that understand what each day is for.
